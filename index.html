Round 01 · Glossary

Race Weekend
& Rules.

The words you'll hear every single race weekend. Hover any box — or tap it on mobile — to reveal what it means and see it in action.

Practice (FP1–FP3)+
Race Weekend

Free practice sessions held Friday and Saturday, where teams test car setup, tyre performance, and fuel loads ahead of qualifying.

ExampleDrivers use FP1–FP3 to fine-tune balance before Saturday's qualifying session.

Qualifying+
Race Weekend

A three-part knockout session (Q1, Q2, Q3) on Saturday that sets Sunday's starting grid, based on each driver's fastest lap.

ExampleIn Q3, the ten fastest drivers from Q2 fight for pole with one final flying lap.

Starting Grid+
Race Weekend

The lineup of cars for the race start, arranged in the order set by qualifying, with pole position at the very front.

ExampleStarting P3 on the grid, a driver aims to gain places at the first corner.

Pole Position+
Race Weekend

The number one starting spot on the grid, awarded to whoever sets the fastest lap in qualifying.

ExampleA stunning final lap in Q3 is often what decides pole position.

Formation Lap+
Race Weekend

A slow warm-up lap before the race start, where drivers heat their tyres and brakes while holding grid order.

ExampleTeams watch tyre temperatures closely during the formation lap before lining up on the grid.

Parc Fermé+
Rules

A period after qualifying and after the race when cars are held under strict rules — teams cannot change setup without a penalty.

ExampleOnce parc fermé begins, engineers can only make small, permitted adjustments to the car.

Pit Stop+
Pit Lane

A stop in the pit lane during the race to change tyres or repair damage — a modern tyre change takes only a few seconds.

ExampleA lightning-fast pit stop can help a driver leapfrog a rival who pits a lap later.

Pit Lane+
Pit Lane

The road beside the main straight where team garages sit, and where cars stop during the race under a strict speed limit.

ExampleDrivers must slow right down the moment they cross the pit lane entry line.

Safety Car+
On Track

A car sent out to slow and bunch up the field after a serious incident, neutralising the race until the track is clear.

ExampleThe Safety Car closes up a big lead in seconds, resetting the race for a restart.

Virtual Safety Car+
On Track

A system that forces every car to slow to a set delta time without physically bunching up behind a car — used for smaller incidents.

ExampleA VSC lets marshals safely clear light debris without needing a full Safety Car period.

Red Flag+
On Track

Signals a full race stoppage, usually for a serious incident, dangerous weather, or a blocked track.

ExampleHeavy rain can trigger a red flag, pausing the race until conditions improve.

Yellow / Blue Flag+
On Track

Yellow warns of a hazard ahead with no overtaking; blue tells a driver a faster car is about to lap them and must be let through.

ExampleA backmarker sees blue flags and moves aside to let the race leader pass.

Grid Penalty+
Rules

A penalty that drops a driver a set number of places on the starting grid, often for a collision or an engine-component change.

ExampleA gearbox change beyond the allowed limit usually costs several grid places.

Points System+
Scoring

Championship points awarded to the top finishers in each race, with more points for higher finishing positions.

ExampleA race winner scores more points than the driver who finishes fifth.

Round 02 · Glossary

The Car
& Strategy.

Now the terms behind the machine and the tactics — how teams squeeze out extra speed, and how it all counts toward the title.

DRS+
On Track

An adjustable rear wing flap that opens on designated straights to cut drag, usable only within one second of the car ahead.

ExampleWithin DRS range on the back straight, the chasing driver opens the flap for extra top speed.

Slipstream+
On Track

Following closely behind another car to reduce air resistance and gain extra speed down a straight.

ExampleUsing the slipstream of the car ahead, a driver launches a move into the final corner.

Halo+
The Car

The protective titanium structure around the cockpit that shields the driver's head from debris and impact.

ExampleThe halo has been credited with protecting drivers in several serious incidents since its introduction.

Car Body & Aerodynamics+
The Car

The wings and shaped surfaces — front wing, rear wing and diffuser — that manage airflow over, under and around the car to generate downforce and stability.

ExampleEven small damage to the front wing or a chip in the diffuser can noticeably change how a car handles through corners.

Power Unit+
The Car

The hybrid engine, combining a combustion engine (ICE) with energy recovery systems (ERS) that harvest and redeploy electrical energy.

ExampleThe ERS stores energy under braking and deploys it for an extra power boost down the straights.

Tyre Compounds+
The Car

Pirelli supplies different rubber compounds — soft, medium, hard, plus intermediates and full wets — each with different grip and durability.

ExampleSoft tyres grip better for a fast lap but wear out sooner than the hards.

Ballast+
The Car

Weight added to the car and positioned to help teams meet the minimum weight limit while fine-tuning balance.

ExampleEngineers place ballast low in the chassis to lower the car's centre of gravity.

Drivers' Championship+
Scoring

The season-long title awarded to the driver who scores the most points across every race.

ExampleConsistent podium finishes all season can build a commanding points lead.

Constructors' Championship+
Scoring

The title awarded to the team whose two drivers combine to score the most points over the season.

ExampleBoth drivers scoring points in the same race gives a big boost to the team's tally.

Fastest Lap Point+
Scoring

A bonus championship point for the driver who sets the fastest lap of the race — but only if they finish in the top ten.

ExampleA late stop for fresh tyres can let a driver snatch the fastest lap point on the very last lap.

Round 04 · Strategy

Every Race Is
A Strategy Puzzle.

Outright speed only tells half the story. When a team pits, which tyre they choose, and how they react to a Safety Car can decide a race just as much as pace does.

The core gamble

Undercut vs Overcut

Two opposite bets teams make on exactly the same problem — when to give up track position for fresher tyres.

The Undercut

Pit earlier than a rival to bolt on fresh tyres while they're still out on worn ones. You lose track position for a lap or two — but faster in- and out-laps on new rubber can let you leapfrog them the moment they finally stop.

The Overcut

Stay out longer than a rival on ageing tyres, banking on a clear track and late pace to gain time while they're stuck in the pits. Your tyres are older — but you're spending more laps in free air instead of traffic.

Planning the stops

One-Stop vs Two-Stop

Before the race even starts, teams model how many pit stops give the fastest route to the flag.

One-Stop

Fewer trips down the pit lane means less time lost stationary. The trade-off: tyres have to survive much longer stints, and pace often fades in the closing laps as the rubber wears out.

Two-Stop (or more)

More time lost in the pits, but drivers spend more of the race on consistently fresher, faster tyres. Often the quicker route round on paper — if track position allows it.

On the pit wall

Reading the race

Strategy isn't fixed on lights-out — teams are constantly re-planning as the race changes around them.

01

Safety Car timing

Pitting under a Safety Car costs far less time than stopping under green-flag racing, so teams often gamble a stop the instant one appears.

02

Track position vs pace

Sometimes it's faster to stay out front on a worn tyre than to have fresher rubber but be stuck behind slower traffic.

03

Weather calls

Getting the tyre choice wrong as rain arrives — or dries up — can gain or lose huge chunks of time in a matter of minutes.

04

Tyre degradation

Every compound loses grip at a different rate on a given track and in given heat — reading that curve correctly is what a strategy call is really built on.

Round 05 · Anatomy

Anatomy Of
An F1 Car.

Eight parts, one machine. Click a marker on the car — or a card on the right — to see them highlight together.

01

Front Wing

The car's first point of contact with the air — it shapes airflow for everything behind it while adding downforce over the front axle.

02

Halo

A titanium ring above the cockpit strong enough to support the weight of a London bus, protecting the driver's head from debris and impact.

03

Cockpit

An ultra-strong carbon-fibre survival cell built to protect the driver in a crash, housing the seat, steering wheel and controls.

04

Sidepod

Houses the radiators that cool the power unit, shaped carefully to manage airflow around the car's midsection.

05

Power Unit

Home to the hybrid power unit — a V6 engine combined with electric motors that recover and redeploy energy.

06

Rear Wing & DRS

Generates rear downforce for stability, with a flap that opens on straights to cut drag and gain top speed.

07

Diffuser

Accelerates airflow under the floor to generate extra downforce without adding the drag a wing would.

08

Tyres

The only part of the car touching the track — compound choice massively shapes every team's race strategy.

Drivers' vs Constructors'

Drivers' Championship

Awarded to the individual driver who scores the most points across the season. This is the title most fans think of first — the personal battle between drivers race after race.

Round 09 · Remembering

The Sport's
Darkest Days.

Racing has always carried real risk. These moments are hard to look back on — but every one of them led directly to changes that keep today's drivers safer. They deserve to be remembered, not glossed over.

Presented briefly and factually, with a focus on the safety legacy each of these left behind.
10 SEPTEMBER 1961 · MONZA

A Dark Afternoon at Monza

Wolfgang von Trips's Ferrari collided with another car early in the race and went into the crowd, killing von Trips and 15 spectators. It remains one of the deadliest days in motorsport history, and a stark reminder of how different circuit safety once was.

Circuit Safety
5 SEPTEMBER 1970 · MONZA

F1's Only Posthumous Champion

Jochen Rindt was killed during practice for the Italian Grand Prix. He had already built up enough of a championship lead that no rival could catch him — making him, to this day, Formula 1's only posthumous World Champion.

In Memoriam
10 SEPTEMBER 1978 · MONZA

The Loss of a Fan Favourite

Ronnie Peterson was involved in a start-line collision and passed away from complications the following day. Known for a spectacular, sideways driving style, he remains one of the most admired drivers of his generation.

In Memoriam
8 MAY 1982 · ZOLDER

Villeneuve's Final Lap

Gilles Villeneuve was killed during qualifying for the Belgian Grand Prix. Fiercely fast and adored by fans, his death remains one of the sport's most mourned losses — his name lives on through Montreal's Circuit Gilles Villeneuve.

In Memoriam
30 APRIL – 1 MAY 1994 · IMOLA

The Weekend That Changed Everything

Roland Ratzenberger died in Saturday qualifying, and three-time champion Ayrton Senna was killed during the race itself the next day. The tragedy triggered the biggest safety overhaul in F1 history — stronger survival cells, better circuit run-off, dedicated FIA safety research, and eventually the halo.

Safety Turning Point
5 OCTOBER 2014 · SUZUKA

The Crash That Brought the Halo

Jules Bianchi crashed into a recovery vehicle in treacherous wet conditions and suffered severe head injuries; he passed away nine months later, the sport's first race-weekend fatality since Imola. His death directly led to the Virtual Safety Car, and accelerated the push toward making the halo mandatory.

Safety Turning Point
Round 10 · History

The Most
Historic Races.

Seventy-six seasons of racing, and a handful of afternoons everyone still talks about. Here are seven of the sport's defining moments.

13 MAY 1950 · SILVERSTONE

The One That Started It All

The very first round of the very first Formula 1 World Championship, held at Silverstone in front of King George VI. Giuseppe Farina took victory in his Alfa Romeo, becoming the sport's first-ever race winner on his way to becoming its first World Champion that same year.

First Ever
4 AUGUST 1957 · NÜRBURGRING

Fangio's Masterpiece

Trailing after a slow pit stop, Juan Manuel Fangio produced one of the most celebrated drives in motorsport history — smashing the lap record over and over to hunt down and pass both Ferraris in the closing laps. The win sealed his fifth and final World Championship.

Legendary Drive
24 OCTOBER 1976 · FUJI SPEEDWAY

Hunt, Lauda, and the Rain

Six weeks after a fiery crash nearly killed him, Niki Lauda withdrew from a rain-soaked, low-visibility finale, judging the conditions too dangerous to continue. James Hunt fought back from outside the points to finish third — just enough to snatch the title by a single point.

Title Decider
30 APRIL – 1 MAY 1994 · IMOLA

The Weekend That Changed Safety

One of the sport's darkest weekends, in which Roland Ratzenberger and three-time champion Ayrton Senna were killed in separate accidents. The tragedy triggered the most sweeping safety overhaul in Formula 1's history — a legacy of research and regulation that still shapes every safety decision the sport makes today.

Safety Legacy
2 NOVEMBER 2008 · INTERLAGOS

Hamilton's Last-Corner Title

Lewis Hamilton needed just one place inside the top five to become champion, and looked set to fall short until he passed Timo Glock at the very last corner of the very last lap, in the rain, to finish fifth and win his first World Championship by a single point.

Last-Lap Drama
12 DECEMBER 2021 · YAS MARINA

The Most Debated Finale

Max Verstappen and Lewis Hamilton arrived level on points for a winner-takes-all finale. A late Safety Car and a contested restart on the race's final lap allowed Verstappen to pass Hamilton and win both the race and his first title — a call fans still argue over today.

Controversial
7 DECEMBER 2025 · YAS MARINA

Norris Holds His Nerve

A three-way title fight between McLaren teammates Lando Norris and Oscar Piastri, and Red Bull's Max Verstappen, went down to the very last race. Verstappen won on the road, but Norris drove a disciplined race to finish third — exactly enough to become World Champion for the first time, by two points.
